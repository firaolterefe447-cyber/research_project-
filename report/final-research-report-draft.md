# Abstract

This mixed-method study will assess the challenges associated with manual attendance management and the perceived benefits and barriers of digital attendance systems at Addis Ababa University (AAU). The study uses a descriptive cross-sectional design with questionnaires, interviews, and classroom observations. Approximately 60–100 students and 5–10 instructors from selected departments are planned participants. Convenience sampling will be used for students, while purposive sampling will support instructor selection. Quantitative responses will be summarized using frequencies, percentages, and mean Likert-scale scores. Qualitative responses and classroom observations will be examined thematically and supported by anonymized evidence. The findings are intended to inform practical decisions concerning instructional time, record accuracy, attendance fraud, privacy, internet access, cost, accessibility, and user acceptance. No empirical results are claimed in this draft until actual data collection, pilot testing, verification, and analysis are complete.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background

Attendance management is part of the administrative processes supporting teaching and learning. Traditional practices, including name calling and paper or sign-in sheets, can require instructional time and produce incomplete or inaccurate records. Large classrooms may increase these difficulties.

Digital attendance systems such as QR codes and RFID systems offer possible improvements in processing speed and record quality. However, technology adoption may be affected by internet availability, device access, cost, privacy, technical support, accessibility, and user acceptance. A context-aware assessment is therefore required before recommending a digital solution for AAU.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Manual attendance management may contribute to instructional time loss, administrative workload, record inaccuracies, difficulties correcting information, and attendance fraud. Digital alternatives may address these problems, but their feasibility is uncertain without evidence about stakeholder needs and implementation barriers. This study addresses that gap in selected AAU departments.

## 1.3 Research Questions

1. What challenges do students and instructors report in manual attendance recording?
2. How do participants perceive the effect of manual attendance on instructional time and academic efficiency?
3. To what extent do students and instructors accept digital attendance systems?
4. What implementation barriers should AAU address when adopting digital attendance?

## 1.4 Objectives

### Main Objective

To assess the challenges of manual attendance management and the potential benefits of digital attendance systems at Addis Ababa University.

### Specific Objectives

1. Identify major challenges of manual attendance recording.
2. Examine its perceived impact on instructional time and academic efficiency.
3. Assess student and instructor acceptance of digital attendance.
4. Identify barriers and recommend practical solutions.

# 2. Research Methodology

## 2.1 Research Design

A descriptive cross-sectional mixed-method design will be used. The quantitative component examines reported experiences and attitudes. The qualitative component explains context, reasons, and implementation concerns through interviews and observations.

## 2.2 Study Setting and Population

The study will be conducted in selected departments at Addis Ababa University. The target population is students and instructors who have experience with manual or digital attendance processes.

## 2.3 Sample and Sampling

The proposal anticipates approximately 60–100 student participants and 5–10 instructor participants. Student respondents will be selected through convenience sampling. Instructors will be selected purposively based on teaching responsibility and experience with attendance systems.

## 2.4 Data Collection Instruments

The instruments include a student questionnaire, an instructor questionnaire, a semi-structured interview guide, and a classroom observation checklist. The interview and open-response codebook will guide thematic analysis.

## 2.5 Pilot Study

A small pilot is to be conducted with 5–8 participants. Researchers will evaluate item clarity, completion time, missing topics, leading language, and technical feasibility. Changes will be documented in a revision log.

## 2.6 Data Collection Procedure

After confirming consent and permission, the team will distribute questionnaires, conduct authorized interviews, and observe agreed classroom sessions without recording personal identifiers. Participation is voluntary.

## 2.7 Data Analysis

Questionnaire data will be summarized with frequencies, percentages, and mean values for each Likert item. Results will be compared between students and instructors where response numbers are sufficient. Interview and open-response data will be coded into categories in the provided codebook. Two researchers will independently test the codebook on a subset and resolve differences.

## 2.8 Reliability and Validity

The questionnaire will be reviewed for item clarity and alignment with objectives. Pilot feedback will support revisions. Qualitative trustworthiness will be supported through a codebook, audit trail, dual coding where feasible, and reporting of anonymized evidence. Quantitative and qualitative findings will be triangulated.

## 2.9 Ethical Considerations

The study will use voluntary participation, informed consent, anonymization, secure data storage, and minimal data collection. Identifiable raw information must remain outside the public repository. Public sharing will occur only after data are cleaned and anonymized.

# 5. Conclusion and Recommendations

**Status:** Recommendations should follow actual findings. Preliminary recommendations for later confirmation include:

1. Pilot a digital system in a limited number of departments before university-wide adoption.
2. Provide alternatives for students with limited devices or internet access.
3. Use identity verification safeguards and test for QR-code abuse.
4. Develop clear privacy, data-retention, correction, and appeal procedures.
5. Train instructors and students before implementation.
6. Provide technical support and an offline/manual fallback.
7. Measure time use, errors, and acceptance during pilot implementation.
